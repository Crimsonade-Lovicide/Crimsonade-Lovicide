# UNBUILT Special No. 3: Frank Lloyd Wright (working title)

**Status:** first draft for review, 29 Sep 2026. Shots planned in `SHOTLIST.md` (about 810 credits, or about 608 lean). Nothing rendered, and no credits spent.
**Runtime:** about 7:45. **Format:** 16:9, 720p (1080p upload master). **Host:** Hugo Ashby, on camera and in VO, voice Arthur.
**Release target (proposed):** Sat 17 Oct 2026, 20:00 UTC, 70 years to the day after Chicago's Frank Lloyd Wright Day, which followed the Mile-High unveiling on 16 Oct 1956. This needs the render approved by about 7 Oct. If it slips, the fallback is Sat 7 Nov.

**YouTube title (proposed):** *Frank Lloyd Wright's 5 Wildest Buildings That Were Never Built*

Alternatives:
- *Frank Lloyd Wright Designed a Mile-High Skyscraper. Here's What Else He Never Got to Build.*
- *The Frank Lloyd Wright Buildings Nobody Would Let Him Build*

Every alternative leads with his name, which is the searchable term (week-one readout, recommendation 3).

**The spine.** Each project is bigger than the last: a mountain, a riverfront, an island, a whole country, then a mile straight up. Under it runs Wright's age, which keeps climbing. He drew the two biggest at 89.

**Built on the week-one readout:**
1. **A payoff inside 10 seconds.** The first line is the mile-high tower, over its image. The title card is 1.5 s, not 4.
2. **The strongest story is No. 5, and it opens on its best image:** Hugo outside the Guggenheim, whose spiral was first drawn for cars on a mountain. No setup comes before it.
3. **Wright's name in the title, and in every Short's title.**

**Tone.** Hugo admires Wright and enjoys his ego, so the jokes land on Wright's certainty, his clients' letters and city committees. **No. 3 (Baghdad) turns serious at the coup:** a 23-year-old king and members of his family were killed. No joke follows it, and nothing violent is shown.

**Legend**
- `SYNC`: Hugo on camera, lip-synced.
- `VO`: Hugo over b-roll.
- **Pace:** about 2.6 words per second.
- **Confidence tags:** `(M)` marks a figure that is single-sourced or approximate; the wording already hedges it. Everything else is H in `RESEARCH.md`.

## No. 5 · THE AUTOMOBILE OBJECTIVE · Sugarloaf Mountain, Maryland, 1924 (0:35–1:50)

**[G1] SYNC** · Fifth Avenue, New York: Hugo on the pavement, the Guggenheim's white spiral filling the frame behind him
> This is the Guggenheim. Its spiral was first drawn in 1924, for cars. On top of a mountain.

**[G2] VO** · Sugarloaf Mountain, Maryland, from the air in autumn; then 1920s motorists on a dirt road below it
> Gordon Strong, a Chicago millionaire, owned a mountain within a drive of Washington. He wanted something on top that motorists would come to see, with, he wrote, "the element of thrill."

**[G3] VO** · the Automobile Objective crowning the summit: a spiralling stepped drum, open cars climbing its outer ramp; then a cutaway to the domed hall inside
> Wright gave him a spiral: drive up one ramp to the top, and come down another beneath it. And inside, under a dome a hundred and fifty feet across, a planetarium, five years before America had one.

**[G4] VO** · Strong at his desk, reading; he slides an engraving of the Tower of Babel into an envelope
> Strong was unmoved. It could stand, he wrote, "on any sloping hill or mere raise of ground." Then he mailed Wright a picture of the Tower of Babel, calling it "the first attempt at an externally ramped automobile observatory."

**[G5] VO** · Wright at his drafting table at night, pen scratching; a snail on the windowsill
> Wright's reply was magnificent. "I have found it hard to look a snail in the face," he wrote, "since I stole the idea of his house from his back."

**[G6] VO** · inside the Guggenheim, looking up the spiral to the skylight; visitors walking down
> Eighteen years later, he turned the spiral upside down and gave it to people on foot. As a Museum of Modern Art curator put it, "The Guggenheim Museum is the Gordon Strong project turned inside out."

**[G7] SYNC** · back on Fifth Avenue, Hugo glances up at the spiral
> So if you've ever walked down the Guggenheim, you've driven down a mountain in Maryland. Slowly, and without the car.

## No. 4 · THE POINT PARK CIVIC CENTER · Pittsburgh, 1947 (1:50–3:05)

**[P1] VO** · Pittsburgh in 1935, an archive photograph: steel mills and smoke along the river, the downtown dim in the haze
> In 1935, reporters asked Frank Lloyd Wright what could be done about Pittsburgh. "It would be cheaper," he said, "to abandon it!"

**[P2] VO** · Kaufmann's department store in the 1940s; then Fallingwater in autumn, the falls beneath the terraces
> Twelve years later, he was drawing its future, for Edgar Kaufmann, the department-store owner who'd commissioned Fallingwater.

**[P3] VO** · the Civic Center at the fork of the rivers: a vast terraced drum, cars spiralling up its flanks; a pavilion at the tip with fish in glass spheres
> At the Point, where two rivers meet, he drew a round building a fifth of a mile across, wrapped in four and a half miles of spiral road. Inside: an opera house, an arena, cinemas, a convention hall and a planetarium. And an aquarium, with the fish in glass spheres.

**[P4] VO** · 1947, a panelled room: businessmen round a drawing eight feet long, one of them tapping a pencil
> He called it "Coney Island in automobile scale." When the city's men asked what it would cost, he reportedly said he didn't care. (M)

**[P5] VO** · the second scheme: a single tower at the Point, two concrete bridges hung from it by cables
> So he tried again: a single tower, about a thousand feet tall, hanging two bridges on cables. His own reported estimates were four hundred million dollars for the first plan and, a bargain, a hundred and fifty for the second. (M)

**[P6] VO** · Point State Park today: lawns, the fountain's jet, the old Fort Pitt Blockhouse
> In June 1948, Kaufmann told him to stop. Pittsburgh built some office towers, a park and a fountain.

**[P7] SYNC** · the fountain
> Which, to be fair, was cheaper than abandoning it.

## No. 3 · THE PLAN FOR GREATER BAGHDAD · Baghdad, 1957 (3:05–4:25)

*Hugo's dryness goes to Wright's ambition only, and stops at the coup.*

**[B1v] VO** · Baghdad, 1957: the Tigris at sunset, palms along the bank, a propeller airliner coming in to land
> In May 1957, Frank Lloyd Wright flew to Baghdad. He was eighty-nine.

**[B1] VO** · Baghdad in 1957: a broad new boulevard, oil-boom construction cranes; then Wright's coloured site plan of the island
> Iraq's Development Board, flush with oil money, had invited the world's great architects. Wright was asked for an opera house. He came back with rather more.

**[B2] VO** · the island in the Tigris from above, low and wild; then the drawing's title block, "Isle of Edena"
> He turned down the site he was offered and asked for an island in the Tigris instead. It belonged to the King, and the King agreed. Its name was Umm al-Khanazir, "Mother of Pigs." Wright renamed it the Isle of Edena, after the Garden of Eden.

**[B3] VO** · the opera on the island at dusk: a domed hall under a sweeping crescent arch, a golden figure on the crown; at the far end, a tall gilded statue on a spiral base
> On it, an opera house under a crescent arch sculpted with scenes from the *Arabian Nights*. On its roof, a golden Aladdin with his lamp, "the symbol of human imagination." And at the island's far end, a statue of Harun al-Rashid, three hundred feet high.

**[B4] VO** · a circular campus, ringed by terraces of parked cars
> He also drew a university. Nobody had asked him to. Walter Gropius had just been hired to build one on the same ground.

**[B5] SYNC** · the archive; Hugo taps the drawing's margin
> Wright noted that the drawings were "voluntarily submitted." Which is one way of putting it.

**[B6] VO** · slow push across the drawing; the music drops out; then an empty palace courtyard in the grey light of dawn
> Then, on the fourteenth of July 1958, the monarchy was overthrown. The twenty-three-year-old King and members of his family were killed.

**[B7] VO** · the Tigris at dusk, the island a dark line in the water
> The new government argued that Iraq needed food, clothing and shelter more than floating gardens and golden fountains. (M) Nine months later, Wright died. He was ninety-one. Nothing of his was built on the island.

**[B8] SYNC** · the archive, quiet
> He drew Baghdad out of a storybook. The real city had its own history to get on with.

## No. 2 · BROADACRE CITY · everywhere, 1932 (4:25–5:35)

**[A1] SYNC** · Rockefeller Center, the towers soaring above Hugo
> In 1935, right here in Rockefeller Center, Frank Lloyd Wright put on show a plan to get rid of places like this.

**[A2] VO** · Depression-era Manhattan, crowds and elevated railways; then the cover of his 1932 book, *The Disappearing City*
> He'd written it up in 1932. "The big city," he declared, "is no longer modern." To look at the plan of one, he wrote, was "to look at the cross section of some fibrous tumor."

**[A3] VO** · winter in the Arizona desert: apprentices in shirtsleeves building a huge model on trestles
> His cure was Broadacre City: a model twelve feet square, built by his apprentices, showing four square miles of countryside for fourteen hundred families.

**[A4] VO** · Broadacre from above: small farms, houses on big plots, little factories, a highway flyover, no downtown at all
> No downtown: just little farms, little factories, little schools and roads, and a car for everyone. The rule was "an acre to the family." By 1945, "an acre to the individual."

**[A5] VO** · postwar suburbs from the air: cul-de-sacs, highways, a car on every drive
> It was never built. But fly over America's suburbs, and you can see why he's often called their prophet. Whether that's a compliment is still argued.

**[A6] SYNC** · Rockefeller Center
> For a quarter of a century, Wright told Americans to spread out. Then, at eighty-nine, he drew the exact opposite.

## No. 1 · THE MILE-HIGH ILLINOIS · Chicago, 1956 (5:35–6:55)

**[M1] SYNC** · the Chicago lakefront at dusk, the skyline behind Hugo
> In 1956, Frank Lloyd Wright offered Chicago a skyscraper a mile high. "The Empire State Building," he said, "would be a mouse by comparison."

**[M2] VO** · the Mile-High over Lake Michigan: a slender tripod blade, cloud at its waist, helicopters circling a landing deck
> Five hundred and twenty-eight storeys. A hundred thousand people. Parking for fifteen thousand cars and a hundred helicopters. And seventy-six elevators, each five storeys tall, which he described as running on "atomic power."

**[M3] VO** · 16 October 1956, a hotel ballroom: the enormous drawing hung floor to ceiling, flashbulbs, Wright with his cane
> He unveiled it in a Chicago ballroom, on a drawing twenty-two feet tall. The next day, the mayor declared Frank Lloyd Wright Day.

**[M4] VO** · Broadacre's farms from No. 2, the tower's shadow falling across them
> The enemy of the city explained himself: "If we're going to have centralization, why not quit fooling around and have it."

**[M5] VO** · a cutaway: elevator cabs stacked five high racing up the core; a crowded lobby
> No client, no site, no budget. And an elevator expert later reckoned it needed well over two hundred elevators. "He wasn't even close." (M)

**[M6] VO** · Dubai at dawn, the Burj Khalifa; then the Mile-High's silhouette set beside it, nearly twice as tall
> The tallest building today, the Burj Khalifa, is eight hundred and twenty-eight metres. Wright's mile is nearly twice that. People see a family resemblance; its architect says he didn't get it from Wright.

**[M7] SYNC** · the lakefront at night, the city lit behind Hugo
> Seventy years later, nobody has built a mile. Not bad for a man who didn't like cities.
