## 1. EIFFEL ENVY (0:30–1:35)

**[E1]** #12, *The Graphic*, 1894: the Wembley tower drawn beside the Eiffel Tower. A slow pan.
> In 1889, Paris opened the Eiffel Tower, and it made money. The people behind London's tower printed the figure themselves: during the Paris exhibition alone, they claimed, it took almost as much as it cost to build.

**[E2]** #21, the 1875 *Vanity Fair* caricature of Watkin. Then a GFX lower third: "Sir Edward Watkin, 1819–1901".

**[E3]** GFX: a map line from Manchester to London to Dover, then dashes under the Channel to Paris.
> And he thought big. He wanted trains running from Manchester to Paris. In the early 1880s he actually started boring a tunnel under the Channel, until the government stopped him. [M on the tunnel's length; the script avoids giving a figure.]

**[E4]** #17, a general view of Wembley Park. A slow push.

**[E5]** #11, the catalogue preface page comparing heights. GFX highlight on the Eiffel line.

## 2. SIXTY-EIGHT DESIGNS (1:35–3:45)

**[D1]** #2, the specification page, full frame, then a push-in on clause 1.
> On the first of November 1889, Watkin's company published a brief. A tower of not less than twelve hundred feet. Five hundred guineas for the best design. And clause one: "The designer will assume that the foundations are perfect."

**[D2]** A beat of silence on the underlined clause.
> Remember that line.

**[D3]** #1, the catalogue title page, then a quick GFX grid of plate thumbnails filling the screen (#10 crops and #1 pages).
> Sixty-eight designs came in, from Britain, America, Germany, Australia, Sweden, Italy, Austria, Turkey and Canada. Someone published the lot as a catalogue, and it survives. That's where nearly every picture in this part comes from.

**[D4]** #6, Design 18. GFX annotations: "spiral road", "railway", "parachute".
> Some of them are wonderful. Number eighteen had a spiral road two and a half miles long winding up the outside, a railway beneath it, and, I'm quoting, "a captive parachute to hold four persons."

**[D5]** #8, Design 29. GFX label: "2,296 FT · GRANITE".
> Number twenty-nine, from an engineer in Rouen named Brunel, was two thousand two hundred and ninety-six feet tall. Made of granite. Nearly two hundred thousand tons of it.

**[D6]** #9, Design 50, with a GFX spiral highlight.
> Number fifty was Gothic, with a steam railway spiralling up the inside: first, second and third class.

**[D7]** Catalogue page for Design 39. A slow pan.
> And one entrant wanted lifts big enough to carry a carriage and a pair of horses.

**[D8]** #5, Design 38: the Egyptian tower with a pyramid on top. A slow tilt up.
> My favourite is number thirty-eight. Two thousand feet, Egyptian, with a scale model of the Great Pyramid on the top. The catalogue calls it "an aerial colony": gardens, a theatre, a museum, a temple, and mansions up in the clean air, renting, it says, at a price "proportionate to their Alpine altitude."

## 4. WHY IT STOPPED (5:20–6:45)

**[W1]** #19, the tower with a crowd. A slow pan.
> People did come. The local historian's figure is a hundred and twenty thousand visitors to the park in 1895. [M] But fewer than Watkin's company had hoped.

**[W2]** GFX quote card: "there was no money left to carry on" (Philip Grant, Brent Archives).
> The money ran out. As the borough's historian puts it, even before Watkin died in 1901, "there was no money left to carry on."

**[W3]** #14, a slow push-in to the base of the legs.
> And then there was the ground. The tower's feet began to sink into the clay. Historians link it to that cost-saving change: four legs instead of eight, each carrying more weight. [M on the cause; the script says "historians link it".]

**[W4]** #2, clause 1 again, the red underline redrawn.
> Assume the foundations are perfect.

**[W5]** #15, the stump about 1900. GFX lower third: "'Watkin's Folly'".
> By 1902 the lifts were judged unsafe and the platform shut. By then it already had a nickname: Watkin's Folly.

**[W6]** GFX timeline: 1889, 1890, 1892, 1896, 1902, 1904, Sept 1907.
> Taking it down started in 1904. In September 1907, they blew the foundations up with dynamite.
