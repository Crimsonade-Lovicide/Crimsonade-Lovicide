## 1. Eiffel envy

**5. [E1]**  In 1889, Paris opened the Eiffel Tower, and it made money. The people behind London's tower printed the figure themselves: during the Paris exhibition alone, they claimed, it took almost as much as it cost to build.

 *(pause)*

**6. [E2]**  One man in London took that personally. Sir Edward Watkin ran railways. He chaired the Metropolitan, the line out of Baker Street, along with several others.

 *(pause)*

**7. [E3]**  And he thought big. He wanted trains running from Manchester to Paris. In the early 1880s he actually started boring a tunnel under the Channel, until the government stopped him.

 *(pause)*

**8. [E4]**  In 1889 he bought a country estate at Wembley Park, about twelve minutes from Baker Street by train. His plan was simple. Build something people would cross England to see, and sell them the ticket to get there.

 *(pause)*

**9. [E5]**  That something would be a tower. Taller than Eiffel's.

 *(pause)*

## 2. Sixty-eight designs

**10. [D1]**  On the first of November 1889, Watkin's company published a brief. A tower of not less than twelve hundred feet. Five hundred guineas for the best design. And clause one: "The designer will assume that the foundations are perfect."

 *(pause)*

**11. [D2]**  Remember that line.

 *(pause)*

**12. [D3]**  Sixty-eight designs came in, from Britain, America, Germany, Australia, Sweden, Italy, Austria, Turkey and Canada. Someone published the lot as a catalogue, and it survives. That's where nearly every picture in this part comes from.

 *(pause)*

**13. [D4]**  Some of them are wonderful. Number eighteen had a spiral road two and a half miles long winding up the outside, a railway beneath it, and, I'm quoting, "a captive parachute to hold four persons."

 *(pause)*

**14. [D5]**  Number twenty-nine, from an engineer in Rouen named Brunel, was two thousand two hundred and ninety-six feet tall. Made of granite. Nearly two hundred thousand tons of it.

 *(pause)*

**15. [D6]**  Number fifty was Gothic, with a steam railway spiralling up the inside: first, second and third class.

 *(pause)*

**16. [D7]**  And one entrant wanted lifts big enough to carry a carriage and a pair of horses.

 *(pause)*

**17. [D8]**  My favourite is number thirty-eight. Two thousand feet, Egyptian, with a scale model of the Great Pyramid on the top. The catalogue calls it "an aerial colony": gardens, a theatre, a museum, a temple, and mansions up in the clean air, renting, it says, at a price "proportionate to their Alpine altitude."

**18. [D9]**  You might have heard this was a tower for vegetarians. This channel said so in a Short last month. That was wrong. The catalogue never mentions it. **20. [B1]**  To put that in today's terms: it would have been taller than the Eiffel Tower is now, and taller than the Shard.

 *(pause)*

**21. [B2]**  Then came the savings. To cut costs, the eight legs became four. The tower that actually went up looked, as Brent's local historian puts it, "a lot like Monsieur Eiffel's."

**22. [B3]**  Work started in 1892. The Engineer magazine covered it the following autumn. In May 1894 the pleasure grounds around it opened: a boating lake, sports fields, a station built to bring the crowds.

**26. [W1]**  People did come. The local historian's figure is a hundred and twenty thousand visitors to the park in 1895.  But fewer than Watkin's company had hoped.

 *(pause)*

**28. [W3]**  And then there was the ground. The tower's feet began to sink into the clay. Historians link it to that cost-saving change: four legs instead of eight, each carrying more weight.

**31. [W6]**  Taking it down started in 1904. In September 1907, they blew the foundations up with dynamite.

## 5. What's there now

**32. [S1]**  Fifteen years later, builders came back to the same hilltop. In this aerial photo from 1922 you can see what they found: the craters where the tower's feet had been. Around them, a new stadium was taking shape.

 *(pause)*

**33. [S2]**  It opened in 1923 as the Empire Stadium. You know it as Wembley.

 *(pause)*

**34. [S3]**  The very first match played there was the 1923 FA Cup Final. One of the teams was West Ham United. They started out as the works team of Thames Iron Works, the shipyard run by Arnold Hills. Pyramid-on-top Hills. Number thirty-eight.

**35. [S4]**  The old stadium was demolished in 2002 and 2003. When the builders lowered the pitch for the new one, they reportedly hit old concrete. It was the foundations of Watkin's tower.
