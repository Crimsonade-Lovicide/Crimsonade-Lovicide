## 1. THE ODDS

**5. [O1]**  Start with the money. Measured by the size of their economies before the war, the Allies outweighed Germany and its partners by more than two to one.

 *(pause)*

**6. [O2]**  By 1944, it was more than three to one. American output alone nearly doubled during the war.

 *(pause)*

**7. [O3]**  The one front where Germany ever had the economic edge was the war against the Soviet Union. And even there, one economic historian calls it finely balanced, right to the end.

 *(pause)*

**8. [O4]**  So a long war against Britain, America and the Soviet Union together was close to unwinnable. If Germany could win anything, it was a short war, in Europe, with Britain out of it early.

 *(pause)*

## 2. THE DOOR: MAY 1940

**9. [D1]**  And there was one moment when Britain nearly got out. May 1940.

 *(pause)*

**10. [D2]**  On the ninth of May, Neville Chamberlain was about to resign as prime minister. The man he wanted to replace him wasn't Winston Churchill. It was his foreign secretary, Lord Halifax. The King wanted Halifax too.

 *(pause)*

**11. [D3]**  Halifax said no. He sat in the House of Lords, and he didn't believe he could lead a war from there. So the next day, the job went to Churchill.

 *(pause)*

**12. [D4]**  Two weeks later, the British army was trapped on the beaches at Dunkirk. And in the War Cabinet, Halifax argued that Britain should at least find out what peace terms it could get, with Mussolini as the go-between.

 *(pause)*

**13. [D5]**  For three days they argued. On the twenty-eighth of May, the rest of the War Cabinet sided with Churchill. One minister who was there wrote in his diary that when Churchill told the wider cabinet Britain would fight on, they cheered.

 *(pause)*

**14. [D6]**  So here's our one change. On the ninth of May, Halifax says yes.

 *(pause)*

**15. [D7]**  We don't know that he'd have made peace. He wanted to explore terms, not surrender. But with him in charge, a deal in the summer of 1940 becomes a real possibility. That's the timeline we'll follow.

 *(pause)*

## 3. A EUROPE AT PEACE

**16. [P1]**  With Britain out, Germany has no war in the west. No bombers overhead. Nothing holding troops back.

 *(pause)*

**17. [P2]**  And Hitler had always meant to go east. In the real war, he invaded the Soviet Union in June 1941. In this one, he does it with everything he has.

 *(pause)*

**18. [P3]**  America is the hardest part to call. In the real war, its help went first to Britain. With Britain out, it has far less reason to fight in Europe, and nowhere to fight from.

 *(pause)*

**19. [P4]**  Does Germany win in the east? Honestly, historians disagree. It was close even in the real war. So from here, we're not going to pretend. We'll do something better. We'll look at what Germany itself planned to do once it won.

 *(pause)*

## 4. THE PLANS

**20. [H1]**  Seven weeks before the invasion, on the second of May 1941, senior German officials met to plan how the army would be fed in the east. Their minutes say that if Germany took what it needed from the country, "tens of millions" of people would "undoubtedly" starve.

 *(pause)*

**21. [H2]**  That wasn't a warning. It was the plan.

 *(pause)*

**22. [G1]**  Then there was the plan for the land itself. They called it the General Plan for the East. One version counted about forty-five million people living there, and called for around thirty-one million of them to be removed, sent east into Siberia over thirty years, to make room for German settlers.

 *(pause)*

**23. [W1]**  In January 1942, at a villa by a lake outside Berlin, senior officials met to coordinate the murder of Europe's Jews. The record of that meeting has a list, country by country. The total is over eleven million.

 *(pause)*

**24. [W2]**  The list includes countries Germany never controlled. England: three hundred and thirty thousand. Ireland: four thousand.

 *(pause)*

**25. [W3]**  In the real war, about six million Jews were murdered. The list shows who was meant to be next.

 *(pause)*

**26. [B1]**  Britain had a list of its own. In 1940, the SS drew up a handbook for the invasion of Britain. At the back are about two thousand eight hundred names of people to be arrested. Churchill is on it.

 *(pause)*

**27. [B2]**  In our timeline, a peace deal means no invasion. But the list tells you how they saw Britain.

 *(pause)*

**28. [G2]**  And at the centre of it all, Berlin. Rebuilt around one enormous avenue, with the great dome at one end and a triumphal arch in the middle. Its new name was to be Germania. Due by 1950.

 *(pause)*

## 5. THE STOP LINE

**29. [X1]**  And that's where the evidence stops. 1950 was their own deadline. Past it, nobody knows, and anyone who tells you otherwise is guessing.

 *(pause)*

## 6. WHAT HAPPENED

**30. [R1]**  In the real world, the war turned. Work on Germania stopped in 1943. The dome was never built.

 *(pause)*

**31. [R2]**  But one piece of it is still there. In 1941, engineers poured a block of concrete weighing about twelve and a half thousand tonnes into the Berlin soil, to test whether the ground could carry the triumphal arch.

 *(pause)*

**32. [R3]**  It sank about eighteen centimetres. The limit was six. The ground couldn't take it.

 *(pause)*

**33. [R4]**  It's still standing, on an ordinary street in Berlin.

 *(pause)*
