## 1. THE ODDS

**[O1]** GFX: the economy chart, 2.4 to 1 rising to 3.3 to 1. **[HISTORY]**
> Start with the money. Before the war, the Allies' economies were more than twice the size of Germany's and its partners'. By 1944, it was more than three to one.

**[O2]** GFX map: the eastern front. **[HISTORY]**
> The one close front was the war against the Soviet Union. So a long war against Britain, America and the Soviets together was close to unwinnable. If Germany could win anything, it was a short war, in Europe, with Britain out of it early.

## 2. THE DOOR: MAY 1940

**[D1]** H01: Lord Halifax. **[HISTORY]**
> And there was one moment when Britain nearly got out. May 1940.

**[D2]** H02: Chamberlain, 1938. **[HISTORY]**
> On the ninth of May, Neville Chamberlain was about to resign as prime minister. The man he wanted to replace him wasn't Winston Churchill. It was his foreign secretary, Lord Halifax. The King wanted Halifax too.

**[D3]** H03: Churchill in the Cabinet Room, 1940. **[HISTORY]**
> Halifax said no. He sat in the House of Lords, and he didn't believe he could lead a war from there. So the next day, the job went to Churchill.

**[D4]** H04: Bray Dunes, near Dunkirk. **[HISTORY]**
> Two weeks later, the British army was trapped on the beaches at Dunkirk. And in the War Cabinet, Halifax argued that Britain should at least find out what peace terms it could get, with Mussolini as the go-between.

**[D5]** H03: across the Cabinet Room. **[HISTORY]**
> For three days they argued. On the twenty-eighth of May, the rest of the War Cabinet sided with Churchill. One minister who was there wrote in his diary that when Churchill told the wider cabinet Britain would fight on, they cheered.

**[D6]** GFX: the timeline splits at 9 May 1940. **[UNHISTORY]**
> So here's our one change. On the ninth of May, Halifax says yes.

**[D7]** GFX: the dashed line runs on. **[UNHISTORY]**
> We don't know that he'd have made peace. He wanted to explore terms, not surrender. But with him in charge, a deal in the summer of 1940 becomes a real possibility. That's the timeline we'll follow.

## 3. A EUROPE AT PEACE

**[P1]** GFX map: Western Europe, "Britain makes peace". **[UNHISTORY]**
> With Britain out, Germany has no war in the west. No bombers overhead. Nothing holding troops back.

**[P2]** E01: a German column, Soviet Union, June 1941. **[UNHISTORY]**

**[P3]** GFX map: the Atlantic. **[UNHISTORY]**
> America is the hardest part to call. In the real war, its help went first to Britain. With Britain out, it has far less reason to fight in Europe, and nowhere to fight from.

**[P4]** GFX: "Does Germany win in the east?" **[UNHISTORY]**
> Does Germany win in the east? Honestly, historians disagree. It was close even in the real war. But that's the question this episode asks, so in our timeline, it does. And from here, we don't have to guess what Germany would do next. We can read it.

## 4. 1950: THE WORLD THEY PLANNED

**[H1]** U15: the key line of the 2 May 1941 minutes. **[HISTORY]**
> Seven weeks before the real invasion, on the second of May 1941, senior German officials met to plan how the army would be fed in the east. Their minutes say that if Germany took what it needed from the country, "tens of millions" of people would "undoubtedly" starve.

**[H2]** U15: the English line, highlighted. **[HISTORY]**
> That wasn't a warning. It was the plan.

**[G1]** GFX map: the General Plan for the East. **[HISTORY]**
> Then there was the plan for the land itself. They called it the General Plan for the East. One version counted about forty-five million people living there, and called for around thirty-one million of them to be removed, sent east into Siberia over thirty years, to make room for German settlers.

**[G2]** GFX: the plan's thirty years as a bar, with 1950 marked less than a third of the way along. **[UNHISTORY]**
> In our timeline, nothing stops it. No Red Army comes back west. By 1950, the plan is less than ten years into its thirty. Everything it was written to do is still ahead.

**[W1]** D03, then D04: the villa at Wannsee, then the protocol's list. **[HISTORY]**
> In January 1942, at a villa by a lake outside Berlin, senior officials met to coordinate the murder of Europe's Jews. The record of that meeting has a list, country by country. The total is over eleven million.

**[W2]** D04: close on "England" and "Irland". **[HISTORY]**
> The list includes countries Germany never controlled. England: three hundred and thirty thousand. Ireland: four thousand.

**[W3]** GFX: plain text on black. **[HISTORY]**
> In the real war, about six million Jews were murdered. The killing stopped because Germany lost.

**[W4]** GFX: plain text on black, with the dashed border. **[UNHISTORY]**
> In our timeline, Germany doesn't lose. There is no liberation in 1945.

**[B1]** D05: Black Book page 32, entry 49 highlighted. **[HISTORY]**
> Britain had a list of its own. In 1940, the SS drew up a handbook for the invasion of Britain. At the back are about two thousand eight hundred names of people to be arrested. Churchill is on it.

**[B2]** GFX map: Britain and the Channel, a continent run from Berlin. **[UNHISTORY]**
> In our timeline, a peace deal means no invasion, and no arrests. But Britain now lives twenty miles across the water from a continent run from Berlin. And its Jews are already counted, on a list in Berlin.

**[A1]** A01: the Einstein letter, August 1939. **[HISTORY]**
> In August 1939, Albert Einstein signed a letter to President Roosevelt warning that Germany might build an atomic bomb. That fear is how America's bomb project began.

**[A2]** A02: Einstein. **[UNHISTORY]**
> In our timeline, America isn't at war in Europe. Does it still build the bomb? Honestly, we don't know. But the fear that started it hasn't gone away. If anything, in this world it's stronger.

**[G3]** G01: the Germania model, the full axis. **[HISTORY]**
> And at the centre of it all, Berlin. Rebuilt around one enormous avenue, with the great dome at one end and a triumphal arch in the middle. Its new name was to be Germania. Due by 1950.

**[T1]** R02: the Schwerbelastungskörper today. **[HISTORY]**
> In 1941, engineers poured a block of concrete weighing about twelve and a half thousand tonnes into the Berlin soil, to test whether the ground could carry that arch.

**[T2]** GFX: a depth gauge, the 6 cm limit against what it sank (18 to 19.5 cm, by source). **[HISTORY]**
> The limit was six centimetres. It sank about three times that.

**[T3]** G01: close on the arch in the model. **[UNHISTORY]**
> So in our timeline, in 1950, a triumphal arch more than a hundred metres tall goes up, on ground that had already failed the test. The capital of a continent, built on soil that couldn't hold it.
