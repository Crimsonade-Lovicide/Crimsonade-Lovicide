## 1. THE NILE (0:25–1:20)

**[E1]** A photograph or portrait of Bartholdi. GFX lower third: "Frédéric Auguste Bartholdi, 1834–1904".
> The sculptor was Frédéric Auguste Bartholdi, from Colmar in Alsace. In 1855, at twenty-one, he went up the Nile for four months with a group of painters.

**[E2]** 1850s–60s photographs of the colossi: Abu Simbel and the Colossi of Memnon. Slow pans.
> He came back obsessed with the colossal statues. He wrote that they seemed to be "still listening to the most remote antiquity." [M: quoted by the Park Service; the original letter isn't identified.]

**[E3]** An engraving of the Suez Canal works. Then a portrait of Isma'il Pasha. GFX lower third: "Isma'il Pasha, Khedive of Egypt".
> Fourteen years later, Egypt was about to open the Suez Canal. Its ruler, the Khedive Isma'il, wanted Egypt to look like a modern country, and Bartholdi had an idea to sell him.

**[E4]** GFX map: Paris, then Port Said.
> He met the Khedive in Paris in 1867, and in 1869 he went out to Egypt with his pitch. [M on the 1867 meeting.]

## 2. THE PITCH (1:20–2:40)

**[P1]** The watercolour, a push-in on the figure.
> A lighthouse in the shape of a woman. An Egyptian peasant, a fellah, in long robes, holding a lamp over the harbour at Port Said. He called it "Egypt Carrying the Light to Asia."

**[P2]** GFX scale drawing: an 86 ft figure on a 48 ft pedestal, with a person for scale.
> The figure would have been eighty-six feet tall, standing on a pedestal of forty-eight.

**[P3]** The watercolour again, or a photograph of a Colmar model if its rights are clear.
> He made clay models and watercolours. Some of them are still in his museum in Colmar.

**[P4]** An engraving of the canal opening, November 1869.
> The answer was no. The canal had cost a fortune, and the Khedive couldn't afford a colossus. According to one account, it was the canal's builder, Ferdinand de Lesseps, who told Bartholdi so. [M on who said it.]

**[P5]** A 19th-century photograph of the Port Said lighthouse.
> So Port Said got a lighthouse, just not his. A plain tower of concrete, fifty-six metres tall, finished in time for the canal's opening in November 1869. It's still standing.

## 3. LIBERTY (2:40–3:50)

**[L1]** A portrait of Laboulaye.
> Bartholdi didn't let go of the idea of a giant. In France, he'd fallen in with Édouard de Laboulaye, a scholar who admired American democracy and wanted France to give America a monument.

**[L2]** GFX map: France to New York, 1871.
> In 1871, Bartholdi sailed to New York to find a site. Within three months he'd picked one: a small island in the harbour, Bedloe's Island.

**[L3]** 1880s photographs of Liberty being built in Paris: the hand, the head, the scaffolded figure.
> Liberty was built in Paris, in thin hammered copper over an iron frame engineered by Gustave Eiffel.

**[L4]** Edward Moran's painting of the 1886 unveiling. Then the LIDAR or a photograph of Liberty today.
> From the top of her base to the tip of the torch she's a hundred and fifty-one feet. She was unveiled on the twenty-eighth of October, 1886.

## 4. THE RUMOUR (3:50–4:50)

**[R1]** The *Springfield Weekly Republican*, 15 October 1886, page 2. GFX highlight, line by line.
> And that same month, the rumour was already in the papers. A paragraph printed across America said Bartholdi had first made the goddess "to represent Commerce", as a lighthouse for the Suez Canal, and that when the Khedive didn't like it, it was "palmed off" as Liberty.

**[R2]** The same page. GFX highlight on "does not at all resemble".
> The same paragraph answered it. Yes, Bartholdi had made a sketch of "a figure of Egypt with the face of a female fellah." But, it said, that sketch "does not at all resemble the one now on Bedloe's island."

**[R3]** GFX: the newspaper page fades to the watercolour.
> So the papers that spread the story also printed the best evidence against it. [YOUR CALL]

## 5. THE VERDICT (4:50–5:40)

**[V1]** GFX: the watercolour and Liberty side by side, drawn to the same scale.
> Put them side by side. A robed woman, a light held high, a colossus at the mouth of a harbour. It's the same idea. But it's a different figure, a different costume and a different face. And the Egypt statue would have been only a little over half Liberty's height.

**[V2]** GFX: the 2015 headline, with the word "peasant" highlighted in the article below it.
> And the version you might have heard, that Liberty was "originally a Muslim woman"? I couldn't find that in any source from the time. It comes from a 2015 headline. The article underneath it says "peasant".

**[V3]** GFX verdict card: HALF TRUE.
> So was Liberty built for Egypt? No. But the idea Bartholdi took to Egypt didn't die there. It changed its clothes and crossed an ocean. [YOUR CALL]

**[T1]** A pre-1925 postcard of the de Lesseps statue at the harbour entrance of Port Said.
