# Research and fact-check: No. 1, Willson's Pyramid (Primrose Hill, 1828–1834)

Part of "Monuments to the Dead" (Halloween special). Researched 27 Sep 2026.

**Confidence levels.** **High**: a primary source, or two or more good sources that agree. **Medium**: one good source, or sources that differ in detail. **Low**: blog-only, single-sourced from a weak source, or contradicted.

**About the "primary" sources.** No scan of Willson's own prospectus is online. Google Books has two catalogue records for it but shows no pages (see Sources). Instead I used contemporary texts that reprint or quote the prospectus, which are nearly as good:
- **PM 1834**: *The Penny Magazine*, 4 Oct 1834, pp. 388–391. This is a detailed summary of the prospectus with direct quotes. The magazine says Willson himself gave them the "Prospectus, and other papers". It is the best proxy for the prospectus.
- **LG 1828**: *The Literary Gazette*, 20 Sep 1828, no. 609. A review of the 1828 exhibit, quoting its catalogue text.
- **RA 1831**: the Royal Academy exhibition catalogue for 1831, no. 991, which includes a sentence by Willson.
- **Lancet 1830**: *The Lancet*, 13 Nov 1830, pp. 234–236. An article signed by Willson himself.
- **WR 1843**: *Westminster Review*, vol. 40 (Aug 1843), "London Churchyards", pp. 179–180. It paraphrases the prospectus, with one direct quote.
- **GM 1830**: J. C. Loudon, *Gardener's Magazine*, vol. 6 (1830), p. 489.

All quotations below were transcribed from Internet Archive OCR. **Before any line goes on screen, check it against the page image** (the links are in Sources).

## Summary

Thomas Willson (c.1780–1866) was a London architect. He was a Royal Academy exhibitor from 1799 and designed many unbuilt national monuments. From about 1827–28 he promoted a stepped granite-faced brick pyramid on or near Primrose Hill to hold the dead of London in sealed brick catacombs.

The scheme existed in **two versions with different numbers**, and popular retellings mix them up:

- **1828 version** (exhibited as a painting with a printed prospectus at the National Repository, King's Mews, Charing Cross): base of **40 acres**, ground line of **1,200 ft**, height of **1,500 ft**, "nearly four times the height of St. Paul's" (LG 1828).
- **1830 version** (the published prospectus, *The Pyramid: A General Metropolitan Cemetery to be Erected in the Vicinity of Primrose Hill*): base of **18 acres**, side of **nearly 900 ft**, and height "considerably above that of St. Paul's". It had **94 stages** (2 below ground and 92 above), **215,296 catacombs** of 24 coffins each, making **5,167,104** coffins, and cost **£2.5 million** (PM 1834; WR 1843; RA 1831). Willson himself wrote in 1830 that he had "simplified, and reduced" the project for economy (Lancet 1830).

The press laughed at it (LG 1828: "monstrous piece of folly"), and garden-cemetery reformers such as Loudon rejected vault burial. The rival General Cemetery Company (the barrister George Frederick Carden's Père-Lachaise-style scheme) got its Act in 1832 and opened Kensal Green in 1833. Willson took Carden's side only briefly: he bought five shares. He then publicly accused Carden of stealing his plans and of offering him committee office if he gave up the pyramid (Lancet 1830). He kept promoting the pyramid (RA 1831; PM 1834) and revived it in 1851. He later worked for the Nunhead cemetery company (1842, by his own court testimony, reported second-hand). He was found to have obtained £200 by fraud in 1853, went bankrupt in 1865, and died in 1866. Nothing was ever built on Primrose Hill, which became a public park in 1842.

## Quotes usable as lines (verbatim, with source)

1. **The mission statement** (prospectus, as quoted in PM 1834, p. 388):
   > "a metropolitan cemetery on a scale commensurate with the necessities of the largest city in the world, embracing prospectively the demands of centuries, sufficiently capacious to receive 5,000,000 of the dead, where they may repose in perfect security, without interfering with the comfort, the health, the business, the property, or the pursuits of the living."

   (WR 1843 prints the same sentence with "five millions of the dead" and without "the property".)

2. **The closing flourish** (prospectus, as quoted in PM 1834, p. 389):
   > "This grand mausoleum … will go far towards completing the glory of London. It will rise in majesty over its splendid fanes and lofty towers,—teaching the living to die, and the dying to live for ever."

3. **The sales pitch for eternity** (prospectus, as quoted in PM 1834, p. 390):
   > "… and could only be affected by the convulsions incident to nature; and may be, therefore, said to be almost as immoveable and imperishable as the globe itself. To trace the length of its shadow at sun-rise and at eve, and to toil up its singular passages to the summit, will beguile the hours of the curious, and impress feelings of solemn awe and admiration on every beholder."

4. **Acres out of thin air** (Willson's catalogue text, RA 1831, no. 991):
   > "The base of the Pyramid comprehends eighteen acres, which being multiplied by the several stages to be erected one above another will generate nearly 1000 acres, self-created out of the void space overhead as the building progresses upwards above the earth."

5. **Willson on his critics** (Lancet, 13 Nov 1830, p. 234):
   > "The novelty of a Mausoleum, upon a scale that contemplated interment of the millions, at once attracted the attention of the public journals, and as is usual with first impressions, met with sarcasm and wit, rather than examination."

   And on Carden's rival scheme (p. 235):
   > "Must the Pyramid sink to prepare the way for a scheme without genius, and a projector without veracity?"

   (The OCR reads "aud a pro-/jector"; corrected to "and".)

## Myths and errors to avoid

1. **"1,500 ft, 40 acres" as the design.** These are the **1828** figures. The published 1830 prospectus has 18 acres, a side of about 900 ft, and a height "considerably above" St Paul's. Don't mix 18 acres with 1,500 ft. If you use 1,500 ft, say it was the first version.
2. **"90 storeys"** (Wikipedia). It was **94 stages**: 92 above ground and 2 below.
3. **"215,219 vaults"** (blogs). It was **215,296 catacombs**. The check is ×24 = 5,167,104.
4. **"Hydraulic lifts" / "steam engines".** Not in any primary summary I found. Only a central shaft "for raising coffins", with ramps in place of stairs.
5. **"Exactly the size of Russell Square."** That was Willson's loose comparison, and the square's garden is only about 6 acres.
6. **"Designed in 1820" or "1824."** ianvisits and Country Life say 1820, and Curl says 1824. Willson himself says 1827. First exhibited in 1828 (a painting). Published in 1830.
7. **"Proposed in 1842"** (Meller). That is wrong. Also wrong: "Willson was a General Cemetery Company board member" (Meller) and "Willson withdrew the pyramid to back Carden" (Country Life). Willson's own 1830 article shows he bought shares, refused to renounce the pyramid, and fell out bitterly with Carden. He still exhibited it in 1831 and promoted it in 1834.
8. **Confusing it with Francis Goodwin's "Grand National Cemetery"** (1830), a classical scheme of about **150 acres** at Primrose Hill funded by £25 shares. Some books merge the two (e.g. *The Open Spaces of London* (1986) describes "a 150-acre cemetery at Primrose Hill, constructed as a pyramid on ninety-four levels"). The "£25 shares" belong to Goodwin's scheme, not Willson's.
9. **"Parliament approved it" or "a company was floated and raised money."** There was no Act and no evidence of capital raised. The Office of Works letter was a courtesy acknowledgement.
10. **"It was rejected because it would collapse the hill."** I found no contemporary source.
11. **"Wilson".** The spelling is Willson.
12. **Walker and cholera as the trigger.** Both come after the 1828–30 proposal. They are context for the crisis, not its cause.
13. **Wikipedia's "Kensal Green (1839)".** Kensal Green opened in 1833.
