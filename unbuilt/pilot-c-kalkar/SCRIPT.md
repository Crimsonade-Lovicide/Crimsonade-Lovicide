## 1. THE PROMISE (0:25–1:15)

**[B1]** ANEFO 1974: the construction site. GFX lower third: "Kalkar, on the Lower Rhine".
> In the 1960s, West Germany wanted a reactor that could make its own fuel.

**[B2]** GFX: a simple diagram, uranium in and more fuel out.
> It's called a fast breeder. As it runs, it turns ordinary uranium into new plutonium fuel. In theory, it makes more fuel than it burns.

**[B3]** GFX map: West Germany, Belgium and the Netherlands, with Kalkar a few kilometres from the Dutch border.
> Germany, Belgium and the Netherlands went into it together. The site they chose was Kalkar, a small town on the Lower Rhine. One reason, according to the project's own history: it was close to the Dutch partner. You could reach it by bike.

**[B4]** ANEFO 1974: the entrance sign, then the cows.
> Building started in April 1973, in the middle of farmland.

> The price was the first thing to go. In 1965, the estimate was three hundred and ten million marks. By 1971, six hundred and seventy million. The contract signed in 1972 said one and a half billion. Ten years later, it was more than six billion.

## 3. THE FARMER (1:50–3:00)

**[F1]** PARK/COMMONS: the Maas farm buildings at Hönnepel (2020 photos, licence to check), or ANEFO's Kalkar market-square demonstration.
> Right next to the site lived a farmer called Josef Maas. His family had farmed there for generations. In 1972, before construction had even started, he went to court.

**[F2]** GFX: a card reading "Federal Constitutional Court, 8 August 1978: the Kalkar decision".
> His case went all the way to Germany's Constitutional Court. In 1978, the court said the breeder was legal. But it also said that a decision this big, whether the country should use nuclear power at all, belongs to Parliament. German law students still learn it as the Kalkar decision.

**[F3]** COMMONS: the police checkpoint on the Rhine bridge at Emmerich (licence to check), then ANEFO: protesters at the site.
> Outside the courts, the fight got bigger. In September 1977, tens of thousands of people marched on Kalkar. Police set up checkpoints on the roads in, including on the Rhine bridge.

**[F4]** ANEFO: the wall being built around the plant, with a protest sign on it.
> And around the reactor, a wall went up.

## 4. THE STATE TURNS (3:00–4:00)

**[S1]** GFX: a stack of permit cards, stamped one by one.
> But the real obstacle wasn't the protesters. It was paperwork. Kalkar was licensed in stages, permit by permit, by the state of North Rhine-Westphalia.

**[S2]** GFX: a date card, November 1984.
> In 1984, there was a sodium fire on the roof of the reactor building.

**[S3]** GFX quote card (not the magazine page itself).
> The next year, the state's politics turned. Friedhelm Farthmann had been the state minister responsible for about a dozen of Kalkar's permits. In 1985, he gave an interview to Der Spiegel. The headline was: "Don't kindle this hell fire."

**[S4]** GFX: a date card, April 1986. A black frame, no Chernobyl footage.
> Then came Chernobyl.

**[S5]** GFX: two court cards, state versus federal government, 1990.
> The state and the federal government ended up in the Constitutional Court again, and in 1990 the federal government won. But the state never signed the last two permits: one to bring in the fuel, and one to switch the reactor on.

## 5. HOT AND WAITING (4:00–4:50)

**[W1]** ANEFO: the finished plant.
> Meanwhile, the plant was finished. In May 1985, its cooling system was filled with liquid sodium, the metal that carries the heat out of a fast breeder.

**[W2]** GFX: a pipe diagram with a temperature readout.
> Letting the sodium freeze, or draining it, risked damaging the plant. So for six years, hot sodium was kept moving through a reactor that had never been switched on.

**[W3]** GFX: a counter ticking up, "105 million marks a year".
> That cost a hundred and five million marks a year.

**[W4]** GFX: 205 fuel elements, split 123 Hanau and 82 Belgium.
> The fuel was waiting too. Two hundred and five plutonium fuel elements had been made for it. A hundred and twenty-three sat in a store at Hanau, and eighty-two in Belgium. Not one was ever loaded.

## 6. THE END (4:50–5:30)

**[E1]** GFX: a date card, 20 March 1991.
> On the twentieth of March 1991, the federal research minister met the power companies and Siemens. They agreed to stop paying. That was the end.

**[E2]** GFX quote card.
> The ministry's press release said the responsibility for the end of Kalkar "clearly lies with the State of North Rhine-Westphalia."

**[E3]** GFX: the Latin, then its translation.
> The project's official history, published by the research centre behind it, ends with a line from Seneca: "The time will come when our descendants will be amazed that we did not know things so plain."

## 7. THE TWIST (5:30–6:35)

**[T1]** COMMONS: the farm today, or the 1980s demonstration photo with Maas, if a free one exists.
> Now go back to the farmer. By 1985, after thirteen years in court, Josef Maas was exhausted and deep in debt. About one and a half million marks. [M]

**[T2]** GFX: the 300 km move, Kalkar to Blomberg, on the map.
> So he sold the family farm. The buyer was the company building the reactor, and the condition was that he drop the lawsuits. The price, he later told a newspaper, was four point three million marks. He moved three hundred kilometres away. [M: the price is from one interview.]

**[T3]** PARK: an aerial view of the site today.
> In 1995, the reactor itself was sold. The buyer was Hennie van der Most, a Dutch businessman who builds amusement parks. From the country you could reach by bike.

**[T4]** GFX: two price tags side by side, "the farm: 4.3 million marks" and "the reactor: ?". [YOUR CALL]
> The price was reported as a few million. By Maas's own account, the Dutchman paid less for the whole reactor than the reactor's builders had paid for his farm. [M: his claim; the reported prices support it.]

**[T5]** PARK: the climbing wall on the cooling tower, the hotels, then the swing.
> Within a few years, it was open: "Nuclear Water Wonderland". Today it's Wunderland Kalkar, with hotels on the site, a climbing wall up the outside of the cooling tower, and, since 2009, a swing ride inside it.
