## Theme A: Palaces kings never got (recommended)

**The spine: how close each came.** It escalates from drawings only, to roads and a model, to a cornerstone, to 105 houses bought and the earth moved, and finally to a Kremlin wall actually torn down. The scale climbs with it, to more than 600 metres.

| # | Entry | How close | The payoff |
|---|---|---|---|
| **5** | **Schinkel's palace on the Acropolis**, for King Otto of Greece (1834) | Drawings | See below |
| **4** | **Ludwig II's Falkenstein** (1883–86) | A road and a water pipe up the mountain, and a model | See below |
| **3** | **Bernini's Louvre** for Louis XIV (1665) | A cornerstone laid at a royal ceremony | See below |
| **2** | **Napoleon's Palace of the King of Rome**, Chaillot hill, Paris (1811–13) | 105 houses bought, earthworks, a 400 m façade | See below |
| **1** | **Bazhenov's Grand Kremlin Palace** for Catherine the Great (1767–75) | Foundation ceremony held; part of the Kremlin wall demolished | See below |
| Stinger | **L'Enfant's "President's Palace"** (1791) | — | See below |

**No. 5 payoff: the Acropolis.** A king's palace built right beside the Parthenon, with the ancient temples kept as garden ornaments. Klenze called it "a great architect's (beautiful) midsummer night's dream." What was built instead is now the Greek Parliament. Four years later Schinkel tried again in Crimea, for the Russian Empress. She asked why he didn't make "a smaller possibility instead of this impossibility… we'd be old before it was finished."

**No. 4 payoff: Falkenstein.** The king kept redesigning one room, his bedroom, until it became a Byzantine church with the bed as its altar. The architect was on the fourth version when the news came that Ludwig was dead. Add a quick montage of his other unbuilt palaces, one Byzantine and one Chinese.

**No. 3 payoff: the Louvre.** "Let no one speak to me of anything small!" A cornerstone was laid in 1665. Two years later Bernini learned the project had been dropped, and Perrault's Colonnade was built instead.

**No. 2 payoff: the King of Rome.** A cooper named Bonvivant wouldn't sell his hut. Napoleon: "It shall remain as a monument of my respect for the laws." In exile, he said he'd "never thought of doing more than preparing the ground." The site is now the Trocadéro, the classic Eiffel Tower view. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights was adopted there in 1948, under Eleanor Roosevelt's chairmanship of the drafting committee. A palace for a dynasty became the place where human rights were proclaimed.

**No. 1 payoff: the Kremlin.** In 1771 Catherine ordered the Kremlin wall along the river "broken down." Three towers came down. There was a 17-metre wooden model, and at the ceremony a speech declared "On this day Moscow is renewed." Then she cancelled it, and the wall went back up. A decade later she demolished Bazhenov's next, nearly finished palace at Tsaritsyno after a single visit.

**Stinger payoff: L'Enfant.** "Even America almost built one." L'Enfant planned a "President's Palace" several times the size of the eventual White House. A commissioner said it suited a "despotic government."

- **Title options:**
  - *Catherine the Great Tore Down the Kremlin Wall for a Palace She Never Built*
  - *Napoleon, Catherine the Great and Mad King Ludwig: 5 Palaces That Were Never Built*
- **Cold open (payoff in about 8 seconds):** "In 1771, Catherine the Great ordered part of the Kremlin wall torn down. Not by an enemy army. For a palace. Which she then never built."
- **Figures to use:**
  - Bazhenov's façade: "more than 600 metres" (sources give 600, 630 and 639; M).
  - Model: over 17 m long (H).
  - Napoleon's façade: 400 m, about the same as Versailles' 402 m. "Rival" is fair; "bigger than Versailles" is not supported.
  - Falkenstein: about 1,270 m up.
- **Myths to avoid:**
  - Napoleon's "a Kremlin a hundred times more beautiful": the source is weak (L).
  - "20,000 rooms" for the Kremlin palace (L).
  - Today's Grand Kremlin Palace (Thon, 1838–49) is a different building. Don't use its image.
  - "Construction began at Falkenstein": only the access road and pipe were built.
- **Check before filming:**
  - Whether the Bazhenov model is back on display at the Shchusev Museum. Its restoration began in 2022.
  - The Herrenchiemsee rights for the Falkenstein designs, if any are shown as archive material.

## Theme B: Washington, D.C. almost built these (for a later special)

**The spine: how close each came,** ending at the President's front door.

| # | Entry | Payoff | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | **John Russell Pope's Lincoln Memorial designs** (1911–12) | Pyramids and a ziggurat for Lincoln. Speaker Cannon: "never… in that God damned swamp" (M). | H |
| 4 | **FDR's "Instant Stonehenge"** (1960–65) | Won a competition: eight concrete slabs, the tallest 167 ft. FDR had asked for a block "about the size of this [desk]… plain without any ornamentation." That block was dedicated in 1965, the year the big design died. | H |
| 3 | **Franklin Webster Smith's "American Acropolis"** (1891–1900) | A Parthenon "one-half larger", sphinx avenues and a copy of the Pyramids. He built a walk-through preview on New York Avenue, and it bankrupted him. | H/M |
| 2 | **Robert Mills's Washington Monument** (adopted 1845) | 600 ft, flat-topped, on a colonnaded pantheon 250 ft across with Washington in a chariot. Twain on the stalled stump: "a factory chimney with the top broken off." | H |
| 1 | **Caroline Harrison's White House palace** (1889–92) | Glass domes and palm gardens. It passed the Senate, then the Speaker blocked it, reportedly over a customs job in Portland, Maine. Her completion date passed, and she died 12 days later. | H/M |

- **Corrections to the brief:**
  - The "1891 model" is really Col. Bingham's 1900 model.
  - The name is Franklin *Webster* Smith.
- **Handle with care:** the east side of the White House was demolished in October 2025 for a ballroom, and it is in the courts. At most, one neutral line.
- **Title:** *The White House Was Almost a Palace: 5 Things Washington, D.C. Almost Built*
